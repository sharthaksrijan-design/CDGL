Extensions & exceptions

RNA replication

RNA replication is the synthesis of new RNA from an RNA template. It is a defining part of the life cycle of many RNA viruses and illustrates a second way, alongside DNA replication, to copy a nucleic-acid sequence.

The RNA-copying enzyme

Most RNA viruses use an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP), also called an RNA replicase, to copy their genomes. The polymerase selects ribonucleotides complementary to the template and extends the new RNA 5′ to 3′. Unlike a DNA genome copied by cellular DNA polymerases, viral RNA replication generally depends on a virus-encoded enzyme and occurs in a protein complex assembled in the host cell.

RNA replication differs from transcription in its product and purpose. Transcription usually makes a set of messenger or functional RNAs from a DNA template; replication produces a new genome-length RNA from an RNA genome. The same polymerase can also synthesize shorter viral messenger RNAs, so the two processes are related but not identical.

Positive- and negative-strand cycles

Positive-sense RNA

A positive-sense genome can usually be translated soon after it reaches the cytoplasm. Viral proteins, including RdRP, are made first. The polymerase then copies the positive strand into a complementary negative-strand intermediate, which serves as a template for many new positive strands. Some viruses also produce shorter subgenomic RNAs to express particular proteins.

Negative-sense and double-stranded RNA

A negative-sense RNA genome is complementary to messenger RNA and cannot be translated directly. These viruses must deliver or encode a polymerase that first makes positive-sense messenger RNA. For genome replication, a full-length complementary antigenome is made and then copied to produce new negative-sense genomes. Double-stranded RNA viruses likewise carry the polymerase needed to transcribe messenger RNA from their genome segments.

Fidelity, variation, and exceptions

Many viral RdRPs lack the extensive proofreading available to cellular DNA polymerases, so copying errors can generate genetic variation. The error rate is not identical across RNA viruses: polymerase structure, genome context, replication speed, and cellular conditions matter. Nidoviruses such as coronaviruses encode an exonuclease proofreading activity that can remove some misincorporated nucleotides and contributes to their ability to maintain relatively large RNA genomes. [Ogando et al., 2019]

Replication also creates opportunities for recombination, template switching, and reassortment when segmented genomes are involved. These processes can change genome combinations in addition to point mutation. They are important in viral evolution, but do not mean every copying event produces a change.

RNA replication is not restricted to viruses: RNA-dependent RNA polymerases also function in RNA-silencing pathways in plants and fungi. Viroids provide a separate case; these small infectious RNAs are copied by host polymerases through rolling-circle mechanisms rather than by a viral RdRP. [Zhu et al., 2023]

Examples and connections

Influenza viruses replicate a segmented negative-sense RNA genome in the nucleus, an unusual location for RNA-virus replication. Many positive-sense viruses replicate in membrane-associated complexes in the cytoplasm. Comparing these systems shows why genome polarity alone does not determine every detail of the life cycle.

RNA replication connects the central dogma to viral genome strategies and to hypotheses about early information systems. In both contexts, the key question is how a sequence can be copied with enough fidelity to persist while still allowing variation.

Experimental evidence: replication intermediates

Researchers infer RNA-genome replication by detecting complementary RNA strands that appear during infection, measuring their timing and abundance, and testing whether their synthesis depends on viral polymerase activity. Strand-specific assays require controls because self-priming and nonspecific amplification can create misleading signals. Genetic disruption of the viral polymerase, biochemical assays, and sequencing of replication products provide complementary tests. The combination supports a template-directed model rather than relying on genome sequence alone. [Zhu et al., 2023]
