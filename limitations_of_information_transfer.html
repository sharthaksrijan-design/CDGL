Extensions & exceptions

Limits of information transfer

The central dogma is a statement about sequence information, not a complete account of every influence inside a cell. Its limits become clearer when sequence copying is separated from regulation, structure, and cause.

What the dogma describes

DNA, RNA, and proteins are polymers whose units form sequences. Crick's central dogma addresses the transfer of that sequence information from one class of molecule to another. The familiar route is DNA replication, transcription from DNA to RNA, and translation from messenger RNA to a protein sequence.

The essential boundary is that sequence information in a protein is not copied back into a nucleic-acid sequence. The dogma does not say that DNA always acts directly, that every gene produces a protein, or that biological processes follow a single linear chain. [Crick, 1970]

Established sequence-transfer pathways

In addition to DNA-to-DNA, DNA-to-RNA, and RNA-to-protein transfer, cells and viruses use RNA-to-DNA transfer during reverse transcription and RNA-to-RNA copying during RNA replication. DNA can also serve as a template for an RNA-dependent pathway indirectly through cellular transcription machinery. Which routes occur depends on the organism and biological system.

These exceptions expand the known paths among nucleic acids; they do not establish a pathway that reads a protein's amino-acid sequence to reconstruct DNA or RNA. Viral life cycles are useful examples because different genome types take different routes to a translatable messenger RNA. See viral genetics and the Baltimore groups.

What it does not claim

It is not a theory of all causation

Genes influence cellular outcomes, but they do so through molecular machinery, regulation, and interactions with the cell and its environment. A protein can regulate transcription, alter chromatin, or catalyze a reaction without transferring its amino-acid sequence into DNA. The direction of a causal effect is not the same thing as the direction of sequence transfer.

It is not a claim that RNA is only a messenger

Ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, catalytic RNA, and regulatory RNA have direct functions of their own. Chemical modifications to DNA and RNA, and epigenetic states that affect gene activity, add information about molecular state without reversing the transfer of sequence information. See the overlooked RNA world and epigenetics.

Evidence and scope

Biochemical assays, genome analysis, and experiments on viral and cellular replication have established the known DNA-to-DNA, DNA-to-RNA, RNA-to-protein, RNA-to-RNA, and RNA-to-DNA routes. No experiment has demonstrated a mechanism that reads a protein's amino-acid sequence to construct a corresponding DNA or RNA sequence. This is an evidence-based boundary of current molecular biology, not a proof that proteins cannot influence gene expression, chromatin, or molecular structure. Those causal influences are well documented but are not sequence copying. [Crick, 1970]

Open boundaries and careful terminology

Protein conformations can sometimes template similar conformations in other protein molecules. This is a genuine form of structural propagation, studied most clearly in prions, but it does not transfer the amino-acid sequence from one protein to another. Using “information” without specifying whether it means sequence, structure, or regulatory state can make unlike processes sound like violations of the same rule. Conformational templating treats that distinction in more detail.

The central dogma remains a compact model of sequence transfer. It is most useful when stated narrowly and read alongside the many processes that control whether, when, and how a sequence is expressed.
