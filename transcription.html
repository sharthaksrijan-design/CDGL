Core process

Transcription

Transcription uses one DNA strand as a template to make an RNA molecule. It is the first step in expressing many genes, and it also produces RNAs that function without becoming proteins.

The template and the transcript

For each gene, only one of the two DNA strands is read as the template strand. RNA polymerase reads that strand 3′ to 5′ and synthesizes RNA 5′ to 3′. The RNA is complementary to the template and, apart from uracil replacing thymine, has the same sequence as the opposite coding strand.

A gene's direction is set by its promoter. Different genes can use opposite DNA strands as their templates, depending on promoter orientation.

Initiation: finding a gene

Promoters and transcription factors

A promoter is a DNA region near a gene where the transcription machinery assembles. Its sequence and nearby regulatory DNA help determine where transcription begins and how frequently it occurs. Transcription factors can help recruit, position, or obstruct RNA polymerase; other proteins alter how accessible the DNA is.

In bacteria, a sigma factor helps RNA polymerase recognize promoter sequences. In eukaryotes, general transcription factors assemble with RNA polymerase II at many protein-coding promoters, while additional factors and regulatory elements tune activity. These are broad patterns rather than rules for every gene: organisms and RNA polymerases use different initiation systems.

Opening the DNA

After the polymerase is positioned, a short stretch of DNA opens to expose the template. The first RNA nucleotides are joined without a primer. Once the polymerase clears the promoter, it enters productive elongation; some initiation factors remain associated, while others are released or recycled.

The three nuclear RNA polymerases

Eukaryotic nuclei use three major RNA polymerases with overlapping but distinct roles. RNA polymerase I transcribes the large precursor that is processed into most ribosomal RNAs. RNA polymerase II makes messenger RNAs and many noncoding RNAs, including most long noncoding RNAs and several small nuclear RNAs. RNA polymerase III transcribes transfer RNAs, 5S ribosomal RNA, and other small RNAs. Their promoters, initiation factors, and regulatory controls differ; mitochondrial transcription uses a separate, evolutionarily distinct system. Bacteria generally use one core RNA polymerase with alternative sigma factors directing promoter recognition.

Enhancers, Mediator, and transcriptional condensates

Enhancers and super-enhancers

Enhancers are cis-regulatory DNA elements that bind transcription factors and can influence promoters from a distance. DNA looping and chromatin organization can bring enhancer-bound factors into contact with promoter-associated machinery. “Super-enhancer” is an operational term for clusters of enhancer-like regions with dense transcription-factor and coactivator occupancy; it is useful for identifying regulatory regions associated with cell identity, but it does not name a separate universal type of enhancer or guarantee that a region has an unusually large causal effect.

Mediator

The Mediator complex interacts with transcription factors and RNA polymerase II machinery and helps integrate regulatory inputs at many promoters. It is not simply a physical cable: its subunits and interactions can influence pre-initiation complex assembly, polymerase activity, and responses to regulatory signals. [Whyte et al., 2013]

Phase separation: a model under active examination

Transcription factors and coactivators can form concentrated nuclear assemblies, and phase separation has been proposed as one mechanism that organizes some of these interactions. Experiments have supported condensate-like behavior in particular systems, but a bright focus or molecular cluster alone does not demonstrate liquid-liquid phase separation or establish that phase separation causes transcription. Concentration, binding interactions, chromatin, and measurement conditions all matter; competing explanations remain under study. [Sabari et al., 2018; McSwiggen et al., 2019]

Elongation: extending RNA

RNA polymerase moves along the template while maintaining a small transcription bubble. It selects ribonucleoside triphosphates complementary to the exposed DNA bases and joins them to the growing RNA. Each addition releases pyrophosphate. The polymerase advances in steps, and the DNA strands re-form behind it as the RNA emerges.

Unlike DNA replication, transcription does not copy an entire chromosome. It produces a defined RNA from a selected region, and a gene can be transcribed repeatedly. RNA polymerase also does not need a primer, although its accuracy and proofreading are not identical to those of replicative DNA polymerases.

Termination and RNA processing

Ending transcription

Termination depends on the organism and polymerase. In bacteria, some RNA sequences form a hairpin that helps release the transcript; in other cases, the Rho protein helps dismantle the transcription complex. For many eukaryotic messenger RNAs, polymerase II transcribes beyond the sequence that will become the RNA's end. The transcript is cleaved and polyadenylated, and polymerase termination follows as a separate step.

Making a mature eukaryotic messenger RNA

Many eukaryotic pre-mRNAs receive a modified guanine 5′ cap, have introns removed by the spliceosome, and gain a poly(A) tail at the 3′ end. These changes support RNA stability, export from the nucleus, and later translation. Alternative splicing can join exons in different combinations, allowing one gene to produce more than one mature RNA.

Processing is not identical for all transcripts. Bacterial messenger RNAs often undergo little processing and can be translated while they are still being transcribed. Ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, and many regulatory RNAs have their own maturation steps.

What transcription produces

Transcription can yield a protein-coding messenger RNA or a functional RNA such as ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, or a regulatory RNA. For a protein-coding message, the next information-transfer step is translation. Which genes are transcribed, and when, is a major point of gene regulation.

Experimental evidence: the transient messenger

Brenner, Jacob, and Meselson used pulse-labeling and phage infection experiments in E. coli to test whether a short-lived RNA carries genetic instructions from DNA to ribosomes. Newly synthesized RNA associated with ribosomes, while the message was complementary to the infecting phage DNA and did not persist as a stable part of the ribosome. The results supported the messenger-RNA model and linked transcription to translation through an intermediate. [Brenner, Jacob & Meselson, 1961]
