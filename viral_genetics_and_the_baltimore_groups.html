Viral genetics and the Baltimore groups

Viruses carry genomes made of DNA or RNA, but every virus must produce messenger RNA that host ribosomes can translate. The Baltimore system groups viruses by genome type and the route they use to reach that message.

Why classify viral genomes?

A viral genome may be double- or single-stranded DNA, double-stranded RNA, or single-stranded RNA of either polarity. Classifying only by the molecule is not enough to explain how viral proteins are produced. The Baltimore framework focuses on the steps from the genome to messenger RNA and, alongside these, the strategy used to copy the genome.

It is a functional scheme, not a modern evolutionary taxonomy. The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) maintains the formal virus taxonomy, which classifies viruses using evolutionary relationships and other genomic and biological evidence. [Baltimore, 1971]

The seven Baltimore groups

Group I — double-stranded DNA (dsDNA): DNA-dependent RNA polymerase transcribes viral DNA into messenger RNA. Many DNA viruses use host machinery; some encode their own transcription or replication enzymes.

Group II — single-stranded DNA (ssDNA): the genome is converted to a double-stranded DNA intermediate, which can then be transcribed and replicated.

Group III — double-stranded RNA (dsRNA): a viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase makes messenger RNA from the double-stranded genome. The enzyme is packaged in the particle because host cells do not have a general cytoplasmic RdRP that can perform this viral task.

Group IV — positive-sense single-stranded RNA (+ssRNA): the genome can usually function as messenger RNA. Translation produces viral proteins, including RdRP, which makes a negative-strand intermediate for genome amplification.

Group V — negative-sense single-stranded RNA (−ssRNA): the genome must first be transcribed into positive-sense messenger RNA by a viral polymerase. A full-length antigenome serves as the replication intermediate.

Group VI — single-stranded RNA with reverse transcription: retroviruses copy RNA into DNA, integrate a DNA copy into the host genome, and use host transcription to make viral messenger and genomic RNAs.

Group VII — double-stranded DNA with reverse transcription: hepadnaviruses make RNA transcripts from a DNA genome; a pregenomic RNA is then reverse-transcribed inside assembling particles to produce DNA genomes.

Genome-to-mRNA pathways and replication

All seven groups solve the same practical problem—making a readable message—but not by one universal route. Some genomes can act directly as messenger RNA; others must first be transcribed, copied into a complementary strand, or passed through a DNA intermediate. This is why the framework is useful for understanding which polymerase activity a virus must encode or carry.

Genome replication may use a different intermediate from the route that makes messenger RNA. For example, a +ssRNA genome can be translated directly but must pass through a negative-strand template to make new genomes. A retroviral RNA genome is reverse-transcribed and integrated, while a hepadnavirus uses reverse transcription inside its replication cycle without relying on the same integration step.

Experimental evidence: tracing viral genome strategies

Virus classification by Baltimore group is grounded in the molecular route from genome to messenger RNA. Researchers identify genome chemistry and strand polarity, detect complementary replication intermediates, and test which polymerase activity is required to produce translatable RNA. In some viruses, purified particles carry the polymerase needed for early transcription; in retroviruses and hepadnaviruses, reverse-transcription intermediates can be detected during replication. These observations support the pathway model, while the group label itself does not replace evolutionary taxonomy. [Baltimore, 1971]

What the framework does—and does not—tell us

The Baltimore groups make information-flow strategies easy to compare, but they do not describe the full life cycle, transmission, host range, or evolutionary history of a virus. Segmented genomes, subgenomic RNAs, alternative splicing, and polymerase “cap-snatching” are examples of details that require more than the group label. Related viruses can also evolve different strategies.

For specific taxonomy and current virus names, consult the ICTV Taxonomy Browser. For pathway details, see reverse transcription and RNA replication.
