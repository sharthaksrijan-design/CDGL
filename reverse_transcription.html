Extensions & exceptions

Reverse transcription

Reverse transcription uses RNA as a template to synthesize DNA. It is central to retrovirus replication, but the same chemistry also appears in cellular enzymes and mobile genetic elements.

From RNA template to DNA copy

A reverse transcriptase is an RNA- or DNA-dependent DNA polymerase: it can copy an RNA template into DNA and, in later steps, copy DNA to make a complementary strand. The RNA-to-DNA route is an established sequence transfer and therefore fits within the central dogma's permitted information pathways.

Retroviral reverse transcriptase has two coordinated activities. Its polymerase site extends DNA from a primer, while its RNase H domain degrades the RNA strand of an RNA-DNA hybrid. Because the enzyme lacks the proofreading exonuclease used by many DNA-replicating polymerases, copying errors and template switches contribute to variation in retroviral populations. [Coffin & Fan, 2016]

The retroviral replication cycle

Copying and integration

In a typical retrovirus, a host transfer RNA carried into the virus particle primes synthesis of a DNA strand using the viral RNA genome as template. RNase H activity removes much of the RNA, and DNA synthesis produces a double-stranded viral DNA molecule. This DNA enters the nucleus, where the viral enzyme integrase inserts it into a host chromosome. The integrated provirus can then be transcribed by host RNA polymerase to produce new viral RNAs and messenger RNAs.

Integration is part of the retroviral life cycle, not an automatic consequence of every reverse-transcription event. The enzyme copies sequence; integrase performs the separate insertion reaction.

Reverse transcription beyond retroviruses

Telomerase is a cellular reverse transcriptase. It carries an RNA template within the enzyme and uses it to extend chromosome ends with telomeric DNA. This is a local maintenance reaction; it does not copy an entire cellular messenger RNA back into a chromosome.

Retrotransposons also copy through an RNA intermediate. LINE-1 elements, for example, are transcribed into RNA and use an encoded reverse transcriptase to make a new DNA copy that may insert elsewhere in the genome. Hepatitis B virus uses reverse transcription of a pregenomic RNA during replication, even though its packaged genome is DNA. These examples show that the pathway is not unique to retroviruses. [Telesnitsky & Goff, 1997]

Why the pathway matters

Reverse transcription helps explain how some viruses reproduce, how retroelements alter genomes, and how telomerase maintains chromosome ends. In the laboratory, reverse transcriptase converts RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA), allowing RNA transcripts to be quantified or sequenced using DNA-based methods. Reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) includes this conversion step before amplification; it is distinct from real-time PCR, which describes how amplification is measured.

The 1970 discovery of reverse transcriptase changed the view that information could travel only from DNA to RNA. It expanded the known routes without demonstrating protein-to-nucleic-acid sequence transfer. [Baltimore, 1970]

Experimental evidence: RNA-dependent DNA synthesis

In 1970, Baltimore and Temin–Mizutani independently reported DNA-polymerase activity associated with RNA tumor-virus particles. The reaction used deoxyribonucleotides and produced DNA in an RNA-dependent manner, providing evidence for an RNA-to-DNA pathway. Later biochemical work identified reverse transcriptase activities and clarified how the viral RNA template is copied. The discovery established a route of nucleic-acid sequence transfer; it did not demonstrate protein-to-nucleic-acid copying. [Baltimore, 1970; Temin & Mizutani, 1970]
