Extensions & exceptions

The overlooked RNA world

RNA is not only a temporary copy between DNA and protein. It can carry genomes, catalyze reactions, build molecular machines, and regulate gene expression.

Beyond messenger RNA

Messenger RNA makes the information-transfer pathway visible, but it is only one part of RNA biology. Transfer RNA reads codons and delivers amino acids; ribosomal RNA forms the structural and catalytic center of the ribosome; small nuclear RNAs help process pre-mRNA. Many other RNAs regulate gene expression or perform specialized tasks without encoding a protein.

RNA is also the genetic material of many viruses. In those systems the sequence can be replicated from RNA to RNA, or reverse-transcribed into DNA. This wider set of roles links translation, RNA replication, and reverse transcription.

The noncoding part of a genome includes regulatory sequences and many RNA genes, but “noncoding” does not mean “functional”: many transcripts have no established function, and an RNA's abundance or evolutionary conservation alone is not sufficient proof of a specific role. Functional evidence usually requires perturbing the RNA or its production and measuring a reproducible molecular or cellular effect.

Structural and catalytic RNA

RNA folds through base pairing, stacking, and long-range contacts. Stems, loops, bulges, and pseudoknots can assemble into complex three-dimensional structures. Metal ions and RNA-binding proteins often stabilize or help organize these folds.

Some RNAs catalyze chemical reactions and are called ribozymes. The ribosome is a major example: peptide-bond formation occurs at a catalytic center formed by ribosomal RNA. RNase P uses RNA to help process transfer-RNA precursors, and self-splicing introns demonstrate that RNA can catalyze its own processing under suitable conditions. These examples do not mean all RNA is catalytic; many RNAs function through structure, binding, or regulation. [Doudna & Cech, 2002]

How ribozymes were discovered

Thomas Cech and colleagues found that an intron in the Tetrahymena ribosomal-RNA precursor could splice itself in vitro without protein, showing that RNA could catalyze a reaction. Sidney Altman's work established that the RNA component of bacterial RNase P was catalytic in precursor-tRNA processing. These independent discoveries overturned the assumption that all biological catalysts must be proteins and were recognized by the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. [Cech, 1982; Guerrier-Takada et al., 1983; Nobel Prize, 1989]

The ribosome's catalytic center

Structural and biochemical analyses located peptide-bond formation in the large subunit's peptidyl-transferase center, whose central catalytic framework is ribosomal RNA. The ribosome is therefore a ribonucleoprotein machine with an RNA-based catalytic core, not a protein enzyme that merely carries passive RNA. The precise reaction is assisted by the ribosome's architecture and the chemistry of the tRNA substrates. [Nissen et al., 2000]

Regulatory RNAs

Small RNAs can guide protein complexes to complementary sequences. In animals, microRNAs often reduce translation or promote decay of target messenger RNAs; small interfering RNAs can direct cleavage of highly complementary targets. In bacteria, small RNAs frequently bind messenger RNA or proteins to alter translation, stability, or stress responses.

PiRNAs associate with PIWI proteins and are especially important in animal germ cells, where they help suppress transposable elements and protect genome integrity. Enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) are transcripts produced at some active enhancer regions. Their abundance can mark enhancer activity, but their independent functions vary and need to be tested rather than inferred from their name.

Other RNAs act as molecular switches. Riboswitches in some bacteria bind metabolites and change RNA folding, which can expose or hide a translation-initiation region or influence transcription termination. Long noncoding RNAs can affect chromatin or organize molecular interactions, although a transcript's length or noncoding status alone does not establish a specific function. Functional claims require experimental evidence that distinguishes direct activity from correlation. [Brennecke et al., 2007]

RNA editing and epitranscriptomic marks

RNA editing changes a nucleotide after transcription. ADAR enzymes convert adenosine to inosine in double-stranded RNA regions; cellular machinery often reads inosine as guanosine, so editing can alter codons, splice sites, RNA structure, or recognition by innate immune sensors. In some tissues, APOBEC1 converts cytidine to uridine in a specific apolipoprotein B messenger RNA, producing a shorter protein isoform.

APOBEC family members should not be treated as interchangeable RNA editors. Several APOBEC3 enzymes act mainly on exposed DNA intermediates during retrovirus replication, where cytidine deamination can contribute to antiviral restriction and mutational footprints. The substrate and biological outcome depend on the particular enzyme and cellular setting. [Nishikura, 2010]

Epitranscriptomic marks are chemical modifications to RNA, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) and pseudouridine, that can influence processing, translation, stability, or interactions with RNA-binding proteins. Modification detection and functional interpretation are active areas of research: the presence of a mark alone does not establish its effect, and modification levels can vary by transcript, cell type, and condition. [Roundtree et al., 2017]

RNA, modification, and biological history

RNA's combination of sequence, folding, and catalytic potential motivates the RNA-world hypothesis: early life may have relied on RNA before DNA became the dominant long-term information store and proteins took on most catalytic roles. Modern ribozymes and the RNA-rich ribosome show that RNA can carry out important chemistry, but they do not by themselves prove that a self-replicating RNA system existed before life as we know it.

Chemical modifications further expand RNA's properties. Modified bases and sugars can alter folding, recognition, decoding, and stability across messenger, transfer, ribosomal, and regulatory RNAs. See molecular structures for further detail and evolutionary origins for the evidence and limits of RNA-world ideas.

Key point

RNA can be information carrier, structural component, catalyst, and regulator. These roles overlap but are not interchangeable. The central dogma describes sequence transfer; it does not reduce every RNA molecule to a simple messenger on the way to protein.
