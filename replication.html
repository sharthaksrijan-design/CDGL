Core process

DNA replication

Before a cell divides, it must copy its DNA so that each daughter cell can inherit a genome. Replication is template-directed, semiconservative, and highly coordinated.

What replication accomplishes

Each original DNA strand serves as a template for a new complementary strand. The result is two DNA molecules, each containing one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand—hence semiconservative replication. Base pairing helps guide copying, but enzymes are needed to open the helix, select nucleotides, and check the new DNA.

Replication usually starts at defined sites called origins and proceeds in both directions from each active origin. This creates moving replication forks where DNA is unwound and copied.

Opening the DNA at an origin

Starting a replication fork

Origin-binding proteins help initiate replication. In bacteria, initiator proteins recognize an origin and recruit helicase. In eukaryotes, origin recognition and licensing proteins mark sites during one part of the cell cycle; other factors activate those sites when DNA synthesis is permitted. Licensing helps ensure that each region is copied once, rather than repeatedly, in a single cell cycle.

In E. coli, the initiator DnaA binds oriC, promotes local opening of an AT-rich region, and helps load the DnaB helicase. Eukaryotic origins are licensed differently: ORC, Cdc6, and Cdt1 load an inactive MCM2–7 helicase double hexamer in G1. At S phase, CDK and DDK activities promote assembly of the active CMG helicase and origin firing. Because MCM loading is blocked after S phase begins, licensing and activation occur in separate cell-cycle windows. Origin use is regulated and not every licensed site fires in every cell cycle. [Bell & Dutta, 2002]

Helicase separates the strands. Single-strand DNA-binding proteins stabilize the exposed templates, while topoisomerases reduce the twisting strain that builds up ahead of the fork as the helix opens.

Building the new strands

Why primers are needed

DNA polymerases cannot start a strand from scratch. Primase first makes a short RNA primer that provides the free 3′-OH required for DNA addition. DNA polymerase then reads the template 3′ to 5′ and extends the new strand 5′ to 3′ by adding complementary deoxyribonucleotides.

Leading and lagging strands

The two template strands run antiparallel, but a replication fork moves in one direction. The leading strand can therefore be synthesized continuously toward the fork. The lagging strand is synthesized away from the fork in short sections called Okazaki fragments, each beginning with a fresh primer. The names describe the geometry of synthesis, not a difference in the final DNA.

Replacing primers and joining fragments

RNA primers are removed and their gaps are filled with DNA. The enzymes responsible differ among organisms: bacteria commonly use DNA polymerase I for primer removal and replacement, while eukaryotes use RNase H, FEN1, and DNA polymerases in a coordinated pathway. DNA ligase seals the remaining nicks, creating a continuous sugar-phosphate backbone. Sliding clamps and their loaders help polymerases remain attached to DNA and work efficiently.

Accuracy, completion, and chromosome ends

Proofreading and repair

Replicative polymerases select bases by complementary pairing, and many can proofread by removing a mispaired nucleotide from the new strand before synthesis continues. Mismatches that escape proofreading can be corrected by mismatch repair; other repair systems address different kinds of DNA damage. Together, polymerase selectivity, proofreading, and repair keep the copying error rate low, though not zero.

Finishing replication

Replication forks eventually meet another fork or reach termination regions. Circular bacterial chromosomes do not have chromosome ends, but linear eukaryotic chromosomes do. Because the final RNA primer on a lagging strand cannot always be replaced with DNA, chromosome ends would shorten over repeated rounds without a solution. Telomerase, an RNA-containing enzyme active in certain cells, extends telomeric DNA and helps address this end-replication problem.

Telomeres are protected by shelterin, a six-protein complex that includes TRF1, TRF2, POT1, TIN2, TPP1, and RAP1. Shelterin binds telomeric repeats, helps form a protected chromosome-end structure, and limits inappropriate activation of DNA-damage responses and end-to-end joining. Telomerase is active in germ cells and some stem cells and is reactivated in many cancers, but telomere shortening is only one contributor to cellular aging; it is not a complete explanation of organismal aging. [de Lange, 2005]

Replication errors that remain in DNA can become mutations after another round of copying. For the consequences and repair pathways, continue to mutations and repair.

Replication stress and fork protection

Replication stress occurs when a fork slows or stalls, for example at DNA lesions, difficult-to-replicate sequences, depleted nucleotide pools, or collisions with transcription machinery. The exposed single-stranded DNA is bound by RPA and can recruit ATR–ATRIP signaling, which helps stabilize stressed forks, restrain cell-cycle progression, and coordinate repair. If a fork is not restarted or protected, it can collapse and produce DNA breaks or rearrangements. ATR signaling is a response network, not a single switch, and its outcomes depend on cell type and the source of stress. [Zeman & Cimprich, 2014]

Experimental evidence: semiconservative copying

Meselson and Stahl tested replication models by labeling E. coli DNA with nitrogen-15, shifting cells to nitrogen-14, and resolving DNA by buoyant density. The intermediate band after one generation and the intermediate-plus-light bands after two generations matched the semiconservative prediction. The full prediction table is in the history chapter. Their result established how parental and newly synthesized strands are distributed during replication; subsequent biochemical and imaging methods have resolved the enzymes and intermediates at individual forks. [Meselson & Stahl, 1958]

What is conserved—and what differs?

The core chemistry is shared: a template is read, a primer provides a starting point, and new DNA is extended 5′ to 3′. The proteins and organization differ. Bacteria generally copy a circular chromosome from one main origin, while eukaryotic chromosomes use many origins and package DNA with histones. Mitochondria and archaea have additional variations. These differences change the cast of machinery, but not the basic logic of template-directed DNA synthesis.
