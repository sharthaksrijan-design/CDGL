Foundations

A history of the central dogma

Trace the discoveries, ideas, and experiments that shaped the modern view of molecular information.

Before the central dogma

The story of genetics starts with the name of Gregor mendel, who was known for his experiments on pea plants and the inheritance of traits. Mendel actively crossed pure and hybrid varities of pea plants over generations and studies the evolution of their phenotypes, leading him to the discovery of alleles(which he then called factors). His experiments revealed reproducible patterns of inheritance, but they did not identify a cellular mechanism or a physical information carrier; those explanations came later from chromosome and molecular genetics. While his paper initially was not taken seriously, it was later independently rediscovered by three botanists. This experiment established the existance of discrete heritable factors associated with certain traits.

Key discoveries

Nuclein and nucleic acid

In 1869, Friedrich Miescher isolated a substance from white blood cells that he called "nuclein," which we now know as DNA. He later switched to salmon sperm, courtsey its big and plentiful nuclei. Later, Altmann purified it from protein and renamed it as nucleic acid.

Levene and Chargaff

Around 1910, Phoebus Levene stated that a DNA was composed of repeating sequences of four nucleotide units( a tetramer). He assumed that the nitrogenous bases were all equal in number. Despite this assumption, he correctly identified the phosphodiester linkage of the DNA backbone. Owing to this simplistic view of the DNA, the scientific community concluded that the DNA was too dull and incapable of carriying information and hence people shifted to investigating proteins as the heteditary material. This hypothesis dominated until the Chargaff's chromatography studies proved that the base composition varies with species.

More precisely, Levene's tetranucleotide hypothesis proposed that DNA consisted of repeating four-nucleotide units with equal amounts of the four bases. It was this proposed regularity—not DNA's actual chemical composition—that encouraged the mistaken idea that DNA lacked sufficient sequence complexity to carry hereditary information.

Chromosomes and inheritance: Sutton, Boveri, and Morgan

In 1902, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri independently connected the behavior of chromosomes during meiosis with Mendel's patterns of inheritance. Homologous chromosomes separate into different gametes, and chromosome pairs can assort independently, providing a cellular mechanism consistent with Mendelian segregation. This chromosome theory did not yet identify DNA as the hereditary molecule; that required later biochemical experiments. [Sutton, 1902]

Thomas Hunt Morgan's work with Drosophila provided evidence that genes are carried on chromosomes. The inheritance of white eye color followed the X chromosome, while linkage and recombination showed that genes on the same chromosome can be inherited together but separated by crossing over. Alfred Sturtevant used recombination frequencies to construct an early genetic map in 1913. [Morgan, 1910; Sturtevant, 1913]

DNA as the hereditary material

Transformation

In 1928, Griffith conducted an experiment that demonstrated the phenomenon of transformation, showing that genetic information could be transferred between bacteria. He worked with two strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae , The pathogenic S strain and the R strain. The S strain contains an outer smooth capsule that prevents it from being detected by the host's immune system. The R strain has a mutation that inhivits the formation of this capsule, hence it being easily detected and phagocytosed by the concerned WBCs. He observed that when Heat killed S bacteria were mixed with Live R strain, They became virulent. He concluded that something passed from the dead S cells to the R ones, thus transforming their virulacy.In 1944, Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty identified DNA as the "transforming principle" responsible for Griffith's observations. They demonstrated that when DNA was removed from the cellular mixture through fractionation, the transformation did not occur, confirming that DNA carried genetic information. However, owing to the simplistic picture of DNA, it was still heavily scrutinised. The main argument was purity, Trace amount of protein might be responsible [Griffith, 1928; Avery, MacLeod & McCarty, 1944]

The last result showed that material from the dead S cells had changed some live R cells into heritable, capsulated forms. Griffith called the phenomenon transformation; he did not identify the transforming molecule. Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty later tested the chemical nature of the active material and found that treatment with DNase abolished transformation, whereas protease and RNase did not under their experimental conditions. The result supported DNA as the transforming principle, while the scope and purity of the preparation continued to be discussed. [Avery, MacLeod & McCarty, 1944]

Hershey and Chase

Hershey and Chase used bacteriophage T2 labeled with radioactive phosphorus-32 in DNA or sulfur-35 in protein. After infection, agitation, and centrifugation, most of the phosphorus label was associated with the bacterial pellet, while most of the sulfur label remained with the phage coats in the supernatant. This supported DNA as the genetic material of T2 phage. The result was powerful evidence, but it should be interpreted alongside transformation and other work rather than as proof that one experiment ended every debate about heredity. [Hershey & Chase, 1952]

Discovery of the structure of DNA

On April 25th 1953, three papers appeared together in Nature : Watson and Crick,one from Wilkins, Stokes and Wilson and one from Franklin and Gosling. Watson and Crick proposed the base pairing (A binds with T, C with G), and that the DNA was a double helical structure. This revelation suggested a physical nature of replication- each strand synthesises a complementary strand of themselves. [Watson & Crick, 1953; Wilkins et al., 1953; Franklin & Gosling, 1953]

Progression of the central dogma

In 1956, Arthur Kornberg and colleagues reported the enzymatic synthesis of DNA using a cell-free system, identifying DNA polymerase activity and establishing a biochemical route for DNA-chain synthesis. A separate question was how parental strands are distributed during replication. Meselson and Stahl grew E. coli in heavy nitrogen-15, shifted the cells to nitrogen-14, and separated extracted DNA by density in a cesium chloride gradient. Their experiment compared explicit predictions of three models:

The first generation distinguished the conservative model from the other two. The second generation distinguished semiconservative replication from the dispersive prediction: the appearance of a separate light band alongside the intermediate band supported semiconservative inheritance. This is an example of a model being tested by comparing its quantitative predictions with measured data, rather than by observing replication directly. [Meselson & Stahl, 1958]

Proteins, Messengers and the code

Genes and proteins

In 1941, Beadle and Tatum proposed the "one gene, one enzyme" hypothesis, which suggested that each gene codes for a specific enzyme. They did so, upon observing that certain Neurospora spores lacked an enzyme needed to grow on minimal media. This idea laid the groundwork for understanding how genetic information is translated into functional proteins. Further down the line, it was established that a change in DNA sequence ultimately changed the protein sequence thus manipulating the function of the concerned. [Beadle & Tatum, 1941]

Decoding the message

In 1961, Nirenberg and Matthaei cracked the first codon of the genetic code. They used a cell-free system to synthesize proteins in the presence of synthetic RNA sequences. By using a poly-U RNA sequence, they observed that the resulting protein was composed entirely of phenylalanine, revealing that the codon UUU codes for phenylalanine. This experiment marked a significant milestone in understanding how nucleotide sequences in DNA and RNA are translated into amino acid sequences in proteins. [Nirenberg & Matthaei, 1961]
