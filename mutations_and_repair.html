Context & consequences

Mutations and repair

Mutations alter genetic sequence; DNA damage is a chemical or structural lesion that may or may not become a mutation. Cells use several repair pathways to preserve genome integrity, but repair is not perfect.

Mutation and DNA damage

A mutation is a lasting change in the nucleotide sequence. It may be a substitution, insertion, deletion, or larger rearrangement. DNA damage refers to a lesion such as a chemically modified base, a bulky adduct, or a break in one or both strands. A lesion can be repaired, tolerated, or copied incorrectly; only if a sequence change becomes fixed does it count as a mutation.

Mutations in cells of the body are somatic; those in cells that contribute to offspring can be germline. Their inheritance and effects therefore differ.

Sources of change

DNA polymerases sometimes incorporate an incorrect nucleotide during replication. Spontaneous chemical changes, including cytosine deamination and oxidative damage, also occur. Ultraviolet radiation can create pyrimidine dimers, while ionizing radiation can cause strand breaks. Some chemicals form bulky DNA adducts or interfere with replication.

Replication errors, damage, and mutagen exposure do not lead to an inevitable mutation: repair and cellular checkpoints intervene. Conversely, accurate repair is not guaranteed. Mutation rates vary among organisms, genomic regions, and cell types.

Repair and quality control

Base and nucleotide excision

Base-excision repair removes selected damaged bases and replaces the resulting gap. Nucleotide-excision repair removes a short stretch of DNA surrounding bulky lesions, including many UV-induced photoproducts. These pathways rely on lesion recognition, excision, DNA synthesis, and ligation.

Mismatch and break repair

Mismatch repair corrects many base-pairing errors that remain after replication. Double-strand breaks can be repaired by homologous recombination, which uses a related DNA molecule as a template, or by end joining, which reconnects DNA ends and may introduce small sequence changes. The pathway used depends on cell type, cell-cycle stage, and break context. [Otarbayev & Myung, 2024]

Repair pathways overlap and are tightly controlled. Persistent lesions can activate checkpoints, alter gene expression, or lead to cell death. A repair pathway can itself be error-prone in particular contexts, trading perfect restoration for rapid survival.

Possible consequences

The effect of a mutation depends on its location and context. A coding change may alter an amino acid, introduce a stop codon, or leave the protein sequence unchanged. A change in regulatory DNA can affect when or where a gene is expressed. Some variants have little measurable effect; others influence cell function, disease risk, or adaptation. Effects can differ between individuals and environments.

DNA repair protects sequence information, but it cannot prevent every mutation. That remaining variation is one source of genetic diversity on which evolution can act. See replication and evolutionary origins.

DNA-damage response networks

Damage sensors activate signaling pathways that coordinate repair with cell-cycle progression. ATM is commonly activated by double-strand breaks, while ATR responds to replication stress and persistent single-stranded DNA. These kinases signal through downstream effectors, including checkpoint kinases, to slow or pause the cell cycle while repair proceeds. The tumor-suppressor protein p53 can induce transient arrest, senescence, or apoptosis depending on damage severity and cellular context. These pathways interact; they are not a simple one-lesion/one-response chain. [Zeman & Cimprich, 2014]

Mutational signatures

Different mutational processes can leave characteristic patterns across cancer genomes. A signature may summarize substitution types in their neighboring sequence context, as well as small insertions, deletions, or other changes. COSMIC catalogs curated signatures associated with processes such as ultraviolet exposure, tobacco mutagens, age-related damage, and APOBEC activity. A signature is a statistical pattern, not a direct record of one exposure: signatures can overlap, mixtures can be difficult to resolve, and some proposed associations require independent biological evidence. [Alexandrov et al., 2020; COSMIC Mutational Signatures]

An example: oxidative DNA damage

Reactive oxygen species can modify DNA bases, producing lesions such as 8-oxoguanine. If the lesion is not removed before replication, it can pair incorrectly and contribute to a base substitution. Base-excision repair includes enzymes that recognize and remove oxidized bases, reducing—but not eliminating—the chance that damage becomes a mutation. This example separates the initial chemical lesion from the later sequence change. [Ayna et al., 2025]
