Context & applications

Applications of the central dogma

Knowledge of how DNA, RNA, and proteins are copied and expressed underlies many tools used in research, biotechnology, and medicine.

Reading and analyzing sequence

DNA sequencing determines the order of nucleotides in a sample, while RNA sequencing can reveal which transcripts are present and in what relative abundance. These data help identify genes, compare variants, study gene expression, and reconstruct relationships among organisms. A sequence result is not an explanation by itself: interpretation depends on the sample, method, reference genome, and biological context.

Protein mass spectrometry and structural methods add complementary information. They can identify proteins, chemical modifications, and molecular interactions that are not directly predictable from DNA sequence alone.

Biotechnology

Recombinant DNA and protein production

Researchers can place a coding sequence under a selected regulatory system and introduce it into bacteria, yeast, or cultured cells to produce RNA or protein. The host's transcription and translation machinery performs the molecular steps, while the engineered construct controls which sequence is expressed and under what conditions.

Gene editing and RNA technologies

CRISPR-based systems can be directed to particular DNA sequences to make targeted changes or alter gene activity. Guide RNAs specify the target, while an associated protein performs the editing or regulatory task. RNA therapeutics take a different approach: they can provide a temporary instruction, alter RNA processing, or reduce production of a selected protein. Lipid nanoparticles are one delivery platform for some nucleic-acid medicines; delivery, stability, and tissue targeting remain important design constraints. [Cullis & Felgner, 2024]

Medicine and diagnostics

Molecular diagnostics can detect a pathogen's nucleic acid, identify a disease-associated variant, or measure RNA expression. The test result must be interpreted alongside clinical findings and the limits of the assay; detecting a sequence does not always establish active disease or predict an individual's outcome.

In December 2023, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved Casgevy, the first FDA-approved therapy using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. It is an ex vivo treatment for eligible patients with sickle cell disease: a patient's blood stem cells are edited to increase fetal hemoglobin production, then returned after conditioning. The example shows how knowledge of gene regulation can be used therapeutically, but it does not make genome editing suitable for every condition. [FDA, 2023]

Research methods

Methods often isolate one stage of information flow. PCR amplifies selected DNA; reverse-transcription PCR first converts RNA to complementary DNA. Reporter genes can measure promoter activity, while ribosome profiling provides a snapshot of ribosome positions on messenger RNAs. Proteomics measures proteins and their modifications. CRISPR screens can perturb many genes to test their roles in a chosen cellular context.

Each method has biases. An observed RNA level does not necessarily predict protein abundance, and a change after gene perturbation does not prove a direct molecular interaction. Combining sequence, expression, perturbation, and protein-level evidence gives a more reliable picture.

Ethical and practical considerations

Applications raise questions about privacy of genomic data, equitable access, informed consent, off-target effects, and long-term monitoring. These issues differ between a diagnostic test, a treatment affecting a patient's cells, and a heritable intervention. Scientific feasibility alone does not settle whether a particular use is justified. Careful oversight and clear communication of uncertainty are essential.
