Foundations

Molecular machinery catalogue

A guide to the enzymes, RNA molecules, and molecular complexes that copy DNA, make RNA, and build proteins.

How to use this catalogue

Each process depends on a team of molecules rather than a single “worker.” Enzymes catalyze reactions, structural components position substrates, and accessory factors improve accuracy or coordinate timing. The examples below focus on widely taught bacterial and eukaryotic systems; names and exact arrangements can vary between organisms.

For the step-by-step sequence of events, see DNA replication, transcription, and translation.

DNA replication machinery

The replication fork

Helicase separates the two DNA strands by disrupting base pairing, opening a moving replication fork. Single-strand DNA-binding proteins (SSB in bacteria; RPA in eukaryotes) coat the exposed strands and limit re-pairing or formation of unwanted structures. Topoisomerases relieve twisting tension ahead of the fork by temporarily cutting and rejoining DNA.

Starting and extending new DNA

Primase makes a short RNA primer because DNA polymerases cannot begin a new strand from nothing: they need an existing 3′-OH group. DNA polymerases add nucleotides to that end, so synthesis proceeds 5′ to 3′ while the template is read 3′ to 5′. The leading strand can be extended continuously toward the fork; the lagging strand is made in short Okazaki fragments.

A sliding clamp keeps a polymerase attached to DNA for longer stretches, and a clamp loader uses energy to place the clamp around DNA. After primers are removed and replaced with DNA, DNA ligase seals remaining breaks in the sugar-phosphate backbone. The exact enzymes that remove primers differ between bacteria and eukaryotes.

Accuracy and coordination

Many replicative DNA polymerases proofread newly added bases using a 3′ to 5′ exonuclease activity. The replisome is the coordinated assembly of polymerases and associated proteins at a replication fork. Its organization allows leading- and lagging-strand synthesis to proceed together, even though the two strands are made in opposite physical directions.

Transcription machinery

RNA polymerase and promoter recognition

RNA polymerase reads a DNA template and links ribonucleotides into RNA. Unlike DNA polymerase, it can begin synthesis without a primer. In bacteria, a sigma factor associates with the core polymerase and helps it recognize promoter sequences; sigma often dissociates after initiation. In eukaryotes, general transcription factors assemble with RNA polymerase II at many protein-coding gene promoters. Specific transcription factors and regulatory DNA can further increase or reduce initiation.

Making and finishing an RNA

As RNA polymerase moves along DNA, it opens a small transcription bubble and extends the RNA 5′ to 3′. The RNA is complementary to the template strand and resembles the coding strand, with uracil in place of thymine. Termination mechanisms vary: bacterial polymerases may stop directly at a terminator or be released with help from the Rho protein; eukaryotic polymerase II transcripts are cleaved and polyadenylated, after which polymerase termination follows.

For many eukaryotic messenger RNAs, processing includes addition of a 5′ cap, removal of introns by the spliceosome, and addition of a 3′ poly(A) tail. These steps affect RNA stability, export, and translation. Not every RNA is translated: ribosomal, transfer, and many regulatory RNAs function as RNA molecules.

Translation machinery

Ribosome, messenger RNA, and transfer RNA

The ribosome is a ribonucleoprotein complex made of a small and a large subunit. The small subunit helps decode the messenger RNA; the large subunit catalyzes peptide-bond formation, with ribosomal RNA forming the catalytic center. A transfer RNA carries an amino acid at one end and presents an anticodon at the other, pairing with a corresponding messenger-RNA codon.

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases attach the correct amino acid to each transfer RNA using ATP. This charging step is a major source of translation accuracy: the ribosome checks codon–anticodon pairing, but it does not independently identify whether the attached amino acid is correct.

Factors that move translation forward

Initiation factors help assemble the ribosome at a start codon and establish the reading frame. Elongation factors deliver charged tRNAs and help the ribosome move along the message, using GTP. Release factors recognize stop codons and trigger release of the completed polypeptide; no tRNA carries a stop-codon amino acid.

After release, the polypeptide folds, sometimes with help from molecular chaperones. It may also be cleaved, chemically modified, or assembled with other protein subunits before becoming functional.

RNA processing and regulation

Processing complexes

The spliceosome removes introns from many eukaryotic precursor RNAs through coordinated RNA–RNA and RNA–protein interactions. Other enzymes add, remove, or edit RNA modifications, while nucleases control RNA turnover. These activities determine which mature RNA molecules persist and where they can be used.

Regulatory molecules

Transcription factors bind DNA to influence RNA production. Small regulatory RNAs can guide proteins to complementary RNA targets, affecting their stability or translation. Regulatory proteins can also bind messenger RNA directly, and protein degradation systems determine how long a protein remains in the cell. The machinery therefore controls information at several stages—not only when a gene is transcribed.

Experimental evidence: reconstituting molecular machinery

Biochemical reconstitution tests whether a defined set of purified components is sufficient to carry out a reaction under controlled conditions. Cell-free transcription and translation systems, purified DNA polymerases, and ribosome structures have helped identify the minimal activities and the roles of accessory factors. Reconstitution does not by itself show that the same components are necessary or arranged identically in a living cell; genetic perturbation, imaging, and in-cell measurements provide complementary evidence.
