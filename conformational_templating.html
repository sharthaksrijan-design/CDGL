Extensions & exceptions

Conformational templating

Some proteins can promote a similar folded state in other copies of the same protein. This process can propagate structural patterns, but it is distinct from copying an amino-acid sequence.

Sequence information and structural state

A protein's amino-acid sequence constrains the conformations it can adopt, but the same sequence may populate more than one structure. In conformational templating, a molecule in one state interacts with a compatible molecule and encourages it to adopt a similar state. The “template” is a three-dimensional arrangement, not a nucleic-acid sequence.

This distinction matters for the central dogma. A protein conformer may transmit a structural state, but it does not provide a base-by-base record from which DNA or RNA can be reconstructed. Structural propagation is therefore not evidence of protein-to-nucleic-acid sequence transfer.

How templating can occur

In a simplified model, a stable misfolded protein binds a normally folded partner. The interaction destabilizes the partner's original conformation and favors conversion into the same alternative state. The converted molecule can then participate in further conversion. In some systems, small assemblies or fibril ends provide the active surface, and fragmentation increases the number of growing ends.

Real systems are more complex than this outline. Folding helpers, protein concentration, cellular compartments, clearance pathways, and other molecules influence whether conversion happens and whether the resulting assemblies persist. A similar-looking aggregate is not automatically self-propagating or transmissible.

Prions and prion-like proteins

Established prions

Prions are infectious agents whose propagation is associated with a misfolded protein conformation rather than a nucleic-acid genome. In mammalian prion diseases, misfolded prion protein can convert the host's normal prion protein into an abnormal state and accumulate in nervous tissue. Different prion conformations can produce distinct biological properties, often described as strains.

Prion-like spread is a more cautious term

Aggregates of proteins such as tau or alpha-synuclein have been reported to seed further misfolding and spread between cells in experimental systems. “Prion-like” describes some shared behaviors; it does not mean that every such protein is an infectious prion or that all human neurodegenerative disease spreads between people. The extent and mechanism of propagation vary by protein and disease. [Glynn et al., 2024]

Some yeast proteins also form self-perpetuating conformations that can be inherited through cell division and alter phenotype. These provide examples of protein-based inheritance without implying that DNA sequence has been rewritten.

Evidence and limits

Researchers test conformational templating by tracking conversion, comparing structural states, measuring seeding activity, and determining whether a phenotype persists through serial propagation. Structural evidence from cryo-electron microscopy can reveal how a protein assembles, while biochemical and cellular experiments test how that assembly affects other molecules. No single assay establishes infectivity, transmission, or disease causation by itself.

Conformational templating broadens what can be inherited or propagated at a cellular level. It does not replace DNA and RNA as sequence-based information carriers. For the distinction between sequence and other meanings of information, see limits of information transfer.
