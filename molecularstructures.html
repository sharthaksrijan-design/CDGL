Foundations

Molecular structures

To accomplish their purpose in the process of central dogma, they have sucummed to specific chemistries. Let us take a look on their molecular structures to understand the nuances of their functions.

DNA

Nucleotides and the sugar-phosphate backbone

DNA is a double-stranded molecule composed of nucleotides, each containing a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar, and one of four nitrogenous bases (adenine, thymine, guanine, or cytosine).

The base joins the C1 of the deoxyribose sugar through a glycosidic linkage at N9(for purines) or N1(for pyrimidines). Purines(Adenine, Guanine) contain two rings, while pyrimidines(Cytosine, Thymine) contain one ring.

The DNA backbone consists of the sugar and the phosphate groups. The phosphate attaches to the 5' carbon of the deoxyribose sugar of the next nucleotide, forming a phosphodiester bond. It is also attached to its parent sugar at the 3'end(Ester linkage). By convention, sequences are always written from 5'(free phosphate) to 3'(free OH).

At the chemical level, a nucleotide is a nucleoside linked to one or more phosphate groups. In a DNA chain, each phosphodiester linkage connects the 3′ oxygen of one sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next nucleotide. This gives a strand direction: its two chemically distinct ends are not interchangeable. The terms 5′ and 3′ refer to carbon positions in the deoxyribose ring.

DNA has a negatively charged phosphate backbone at physiological pH. Counterions and positively charged protein surfaces partly screen the phosphate charges, while the bases are largely buried inside the double helix. This arrangement supports a stable, water-soluble polymer while leaving chemically distinctive edges of the bases accessible in the grooves.

Base pairing and antiparallel strands

Point to be noted is that prokaryotic DNA is circular. Hence, it has no ends as such. A pairs with T through two hydrogen bonds, while C and G do so by 3. What this also means is that the ratio of the pyrimidines to purines in a double stranded DNA is always 1:1. Two Antiparallel DNA strands, ones 5' faces the others 3' exist in paired double helix.

Complementarity is a consequence of base-pair geometry as well as hydrogen bonding: a purine pairs with a pyrimidine, keeping the helix close to a uniform width. In double-stranded DNA, Chargaff's equalities (A≈T and G≈C) follow from this pairing, so total purines equal total pyrimidines. The equality applies to the paired strands together, not necessarily to either strand considered alone.

Antiparallel means that one strand runs 5′ to 3′ while its partner runs 3′ to 5′. The hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors that specify a base pair face inward; other chemical groups on the bases are exposed at the major and minor grooves. Many DNA-binding proteins recognize sequence and shape through these groove-facing chemical patterns, often reading the major groove without unwinding the helix.

The word “prokaryotic” groups bacteria and archaea together, but chromosome topology is not identical across all members. Many bacterial chromosomes are circular, while linear chromosomes occur in some bacteria and archaea; plasmids and organellar genomes also vary. Circular DNA has no free strand ends, but linear chromosomes do, and a nick or break changes the topology of a circular molecule.

Helical forms and structural variation

Canonical B-DNA is a right handed helix with about 10.5 base pairs per turn. The helix is about 20 Å in diameter with a rise of 0.34 nm per base pair. The angle between the sugars on the two edges create a minor groove on one side and a major groove on the other side. This acts as a site for protein attachment during transcription and replication.

DNA has 3 families, and its structure varies with sequence and enviroments. B-DNA is the most common form and has about 10.5 base pairs per turn, a pitch of 0.34nm and a deep major groove. A-DNA is a right handed with 11 base pairs per turn, a pitch of 0.25 nm, and a shallow major groove. The base pairs tilt at about 20 degrees from the axis . It is more compact that B-DNA and is common for RNA helices.The Z-DNA is left handed with 12 base pairs per turn, a pitch of 4.5 nm, and a deep major groove. Its backbone zigzags, and it is only formed under high salt and high G,C sequences.

In addition to the original text above, the numerical terms need to be distinguished: rise is the axial spacing per base pair, whereas pitch is the axial distance per full turn. Typical B-DNA has a rise near 0.34 nm and a pitch near 3.4 nm; A-DNA has a rise near 0.26 nm and a pitch near 2.8 nm. The A-DNA major groove is deep and narrow, while its minor groove is broad and shallow. Z-DNA has a left-handed helix and a pitch near 4.5 nm. In Z-DNA the major groove is flattened rather than deep; its minor groove is narrow and deep. These conformations are dynamic structural states, not three unrelated chemical kinds of DNA.

B-DNA is favored under ordinary hydrated cellular conditions. A-form geometry is characteristic of double-stranded RNA and RNA-DNA hybrids, and can also occur in DNA under particular conditions. Z-DNA is favored by alternating purine-pyrimidine sequences, especially alternating CG, and can be promoted by negative supercoiling, salt conditions, or cytosine methylation. It is not limited to high salt and high GC content. Local sequence, hydration, ions, supercoiling, and bound proteins all influence which conformation is accessible. [1]–[3]

Other noncanonical structures can form in suitable sequence contexts, including G-quadruplexes, cruciforms, triplexes, and locally melted DNA. Their formation depends on sequence and cellular conditions; observing that a sequence can adopt a structure in vitro does not by itself establish how often that structure forms or what role it has in a living cell.

DNA topology, supercoiling, and stability

The DNA is tightly coiled and supercoiled in the nucleus of the cell. The linking number is the total number of times one strand wraps the other in a closed molecule.

The DNA is stabilised by base stacking, Hydrogen bonds, electrostatic and van der waals interactions. Their relative strengths and stability contributions are highly debated. Higher G-C content usually leads to higher stability of the strand.

For a covalently closed double-stranded DNA molecule, linking number (Lk) is a topological invariant: it can change only if a strand is broken and rejoined. It is related to twist (Tw), the local winding of the two strands around the helix axis, and writhe (Wr), the coiling of that axis in space, by Lk = Tw + Wr. A linear molecule with free ends does not have the same fixed linking-number constraint. Topoisomerases change DNA topology by transiently cutting and resealing DNA; this is essential when replication and transcription generate torsional strain.

DNA stability is not supplied by hydrogen bonds alone. Base stacking, solvent interactions, ions, and sequence-dependent geometry all contribute to duplex energetics. GC-rich duplexes often have a higher melting temperature than AT-rich duplexes of the same length and comparable sequence context, but GC percentage alone is not a complete predictor: nearest-neighbor order, salt concentration, mismatches, and strand concentration also matter.

DNA thermodynamics and melting temperature

Duplex formation is governed by changes in Gibbs free energy, commonly written ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Base pairing and stacking contribute favorable enthalpic interactions, while bringing two flexible strands into an ordered duplex changes entropy; solvent, ions, and sequence also contribute. A duplex can melt as temperature increases, and its melting temperature (Tm) is the condition at which half the molecules are duplex and half are separated under the specified solution conditions.

Nearest-neighbor models estimate duplex stability from the identity and order of adjacent base pairs, rather than treating every A–T or G–C pair as identical. Tm therefore depends on oligonucleotide sequence and length as well as salt, strand concentration, mismatches, and chemical modifications. A quoted Tm is meaningful only with its conditions stated; it is not an intrinsic constant of a short DNA sequence. [14]

Common forms of DNA damage

DNA can be chemically altered even without an external mutagen. Hydrolysis can remove a purine base, leaving an abasic site; cytosine deamination produces uracil, while deamination of 5-methylcytosine can produce thymine. Reactive oxygen species can generate lesions such as 8-oxoguanine. Ultraviolet radiation can create covalent pyrimidine photoproducts, including cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and 6–4 photoproducts, which distort the helix and interfere with replication or transcription.

These are lesions, not automatically mutations: repair can remove them before copying, while an unrepaired or misrepaired lesion may become a permanent sequence change. See mutations and repair for the pathways that recognize and process DNA damage. [15]

Chromatin and nucleosomes

DNA is about 2 metres in length and to be contained in nucleus, it needs to be heavily supercoiled. About 146 base pairs of DNA wraps in roughly 1.67 left handed turns around an octamer of histones(2* H2A,H2B,H3,H4). Histones are basic spherical proteins that help stabilise the DNA and coil it. This structure is referred to as the Nucleosome. Nucleosomes coil into a 30nm fibre , with the DNA compacted 50x.

The histone octamer contains two copies of each core histone: H2A, H2B, H3, and H4. The nucleosome core particle is commonly described as wrapping about 147 base pairs in approximately 1.65 left-handed turns. Flexible histone tails extend from the core and can be chemically modified; these modifications, together with histone variants and DNA-binding proteins, influence chromatin accessibility and interactions. Linker DNA connects neighboring nucleosomes, and linker histone H1 can help organize this chromatin.

The approximately two-metre DNA estimate refers to the total DNA in a diploid human somatic cell, not to every organism or every cell type. Also, the 30-nm fiber is not considered a universal, regular level of chromatin packing in living cells. Imaging of native chromatin supports a heterogeneous and irregular organization whose compaction varies by region and cellular state. Nucleosomes remain a fundamental level of packaging, but chromatin is not simply a uniform stack of fibers. [4] [5]

RNA structure

Ribose chemistry and RNA folding

In RNA, Thymine is replaces by Uracil. The 2-OH present in RNA makes it more susceptible to hydrolysis, prevents the B-form helix, and enables tertiary structures through hydrogen bonding. It attacks the neighbouring phosphorus forming a 2',3'-cyclic phosphate.

The 2′-OH can act as an intramolecular nucleophile: under suitable conditions it attacks the adjacent phosphate, first producing a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate intermediate and cleaving the backbone. This chemistry contributes to RNA's susceptibility to cleavage, but RNA lifetime also depends on sequence, folding, protein binding, localization, and cellular nucleases.

The phrase “prevents the B-form helix” is a useful shorthand for canonical duplexes: RNA-RNA duplexes and RNA-DNA hybrids generally adopt A-form geometry because the 2′-OH changes the steric and conformational preferences of the sugar-phosphate backbone. Single-stranded RNA can contain short helices, loops, bulges, and long-range contacts; tertiary structure emerges from combinations of base pairing, base stacking, ions, and RNA-binding proteins. [6]

Thymine is basically methylated Uracil. In DNA, a deaminated cytosine becomes uracil and signals damage . In RNA the same change gives a normal base. Much of RNA structure is quite similar to that of DNA. Most of RNA is single stranded, the double stranded ones being either viral or an immune framework of RNA interference. It can also arise if both DNA strands get transcrived on the offchance.

Uracil is a normal RNA base, but its appearance in DNA can be recognized as damage because it may result from cytosine deamination; DNA glycosylases can initiate base-excision repair by removing uracil from DNA. In RNA, C-to-U changes can still alter a message or RNA function, and regulated RNA editing is distinct from spontaneous damage. Double-stranded RNA also occurs as replication intermediates, in some viral genomes, and in cellular regulatory pathways. Transcription from both DNA strands does not automatically make a duplex: the RNAs must be complementary over an overlapping region and remain paired long enough to form double-stranded RNA.

Messenger, transfer, ribosomal, and other RNAs

There consist of three kinds of RNA. mRNA or messenger RNA is the product of transcription. tRNA is a factor of translation as it holds the amino acids through its anti codon loop. rRNA also is involved in translation. Their structural details have been discussed under the topic Translation.

These are three major RNA classes, not the only kinds. Messenger RNA carries coding information; transfer RNA has a compact L-shaped three-dimensional fold that positions its anticodon and amino-acid attachment site; ribosomal RNA forms the structural and catalytic core of the ribosome. Other classes include small nuclear and nucleolar RNAs, microRNAs, long noncoding RNAs, and catalytic RNAs. RNA structure is closely tied to function: a change in a stem, loop, or tertiary contact can affect recognition, catalysis, processing, or stability.

RNA ends, stability, and chemical modification

The RNA is protected by two features. a 5'm7G cap blocks exonuclease Xrn1, and a long 3' poly A tail(over 250 in mammels).

In many eukaryotic messenger RNAs, the 5′ cap is a 7-methylguanosine joined to the first transcribed nucleotide through an unusual 5′-to-5′ triphosphate linkage. The cap supports translation initiation and RNA processing, and protects the 5′ end from degradation; decapping can expose the RNA to 5′-to-3′ exonucleases such as XRN1. Poly(A)-tail length is variable across organisms, transcripts, cell types, and stages of an RNA's life. It can promote stability and translation, then shorten during regulated turnover; neither cap nor tail is a universal feature of every RNA.

RNA carry over 150 chemical modifications, mostly among the non coding regions. These modifications are what justify the existance of intermediated in the Central Dogma since DNA cannot be modifies as such. These modifications assist in active regulation of the processes of Expression .

RNA modifications include methylated bases and sugars, pseudouridine, and many other chemical forms. They occur in tRNA, rRNA, and messenger and regulatory RNAs, and can affect folding, decoding, processing, localization, and lifetime. The catalog continues to expand as detection methods improve; databases such as MODOMICS document known modified nucleosides and their pathways. [7]

For precision, DNA is also chemically modified—for example, cytosine methylation is a widespread regulatory mark, and other modifications occur in particular organisms or contexts. RNA's existence as an intermediate is not explained by RNA modification: it follows from the sequence-transfer pathway in which DNA is transcribed into RNA. Modifications add another layer that can tune how an RNA is processed and used.

RNA structure is investigated with complementary methods. Chemical probes such as SHAPE reagents report on local backbone flexibility, while dimethyl sulfate preferentially reacts with accessible positions on particular bases. Sequencing-based readouts can map these reactivities across many molecules, and NMR, X-ray crystallography, and cryo-electron microscopy can resolve atomic or near-atomic details for selected RNAs and complexes. Chemical-probing data report accessibility and dynamics, not a direct photograph of one unique structure; models combine these measurements with base-pairing and other constraints. [13]

Protein Structure

Amino-acid building blocks

Proteins are polymers of Amino acids. An amino acid is a compound that has an alpha-ammine group and an alpha-carboxyl group with a vairable R group.

At physiological pH, amino acids generally occur as zwitterions, with a protonated amino group and a deprotonated carboxyl group; the side chain determines additional charge, polarity, and chemical reactivity. Those side-chain properties influence how a polypeptide interacts with water, ions, other molecules, and itself.

There are about 20 amino acids in nature, all L isomers. Essential amino acids are those which cannot be synthesised by the human body. Hence, they need to be sourced from outside. Some proteins are conditionally essential , depending on metabolic state. A young child may lack the enzyme to make tyrosine from phenylalanine.

Of the 20 standard protein-building amino acids, 19 have the L configuration; glycine is achiral and therefore has no L or D stereoisomer. Selenocysteine is incorporated into selected proteins in many organisms, and pyrrolysine is used in a smaller set of organisms. “Essential” describes an amino acid that must be supplied in the diet in a particular organism, not a protein. Tyrosine is normally made from phenylalanine; when this conversion is impaired, including in phenylketonuria, tyrosine can become conditionally essential. [8] [9]

Peptide bonds and chain direction

A peptide bond forms by condensation of one amino acid's ammine group and another's carboxyl releasing water. This bond is planer and rigid(40% covalent character). Almost all peptide bonds are trans. Proteins are usually unbranched, The N terminus has a free ammine group and the C has a free carboxyl group.

The peptide bond is an amide bond with partial double-bond character arising from electron delocalization; this restricts rotation and keeps the peptide group close to planar. The trans configuration is strongly favored, although bonds preceding proline can more often occupy the cis state. In ribosomal protein synthesis, peptide bonds form by transfer of the growing chain from a peptidyl-tRNA to the amino group of an aminoacyl-tRNA; the catalytic center is formed by ribosomal RNA. The N-to-C direction of the chain is maintained as each new amino acid is added to the C terminus.

The description “40% covalent character” in the original text is not a standard general measurement of peptide-bond planarity. The chemically useful point is that resonance gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character and restricts rotation.

“Condensation” describes the net chemical relationship between amino-acid units, but the ribosome does not join two free amino acids by simply removing water. The activated ester linkage between the growing peptide and its tRNA supplies the chemistry for peptide-bond formation.

Levels of protein structure

Four levels of protein structure exist: primary(The linear sequence of amino acids), Secondary(Localised patterns, mainly alpha helices and beta sheets, stabilised by backbone hydrogen bonds),Tertiary(Their 3d arrangement held by hydrophobic interactions, ionic bridges, H bonds and Van Der Waals intersctions, fibrous and globular) and Quarternary(Assembly of multiple subunits, Haemoglobin is a alpha2beta2 tetramer).

Primary structure is the amino-acid sequence; some descriptions also specify covalent connectivity such as disulfide bonds. Secondary structure describes local backbone conformations; alpha helices and beta sheets are stabilized mainly by hydrogen bonds between backbone groups. In a common alpha helix, backbone hydrogen bonds form between residues four positions apart; beta strands align side by side, with hydrogen bonding patterns that can be parallel or antiparallel. Turns and loops connect regular elements and often form part of functional sites.

Tertiary structure is the three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide, including its domains and the packing of secondary-structure elements. Hydrophobic burial is a major organizing influence in many soluble proteins, while hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, van der Waals contacts, disulfide bonds, metal ions, and bound cofactors contribute to stability and function in context-dependent ways. Quaternary structure describes the association of multiple polypeptide chains. Hemoglobin is a familiar alpha2beta2 example.

Fibrous and globular describe broad shape and material properties rather than separate levels of structure: many fibrous proteins form extended assemblies, while globular proteins often have compact folds, but real proteins span a continuum. Protein structure is also dynamic. A structure determined by X-ray crystallography, NMR, or cryo-electron microscopy is an experimentally supported model of one or more populated states, not necessarily a rigid picture of every conformation in solution. [10]

Intrinsically disordered proteins and regions

Not every protein adopts one stable, compact structure under physiological conditions. Intrinsically disordered proteins and regions occupy dynamic ensembles of conformations, often enriched in polar or charged residues and depleted of hydrophobic residues that would form a conventional core. Their flexibility can support transient interactions, multiple binding partners, and regulation by post-translational modifications. The transactivation region of p53 is one well-studied example of a disordered regulatory region.

Disorder does not mean lack of function or structure under all conditions. Some disordered regions fold upon binding a partner, and some proteins combine structured domains with disordered segments. These cases complicate a simple sequence → one fold → one function picture: sequence can specify an ensemble and interaction potential as well as a single stable structure. [16]

Folding, stability, and denaturation

Protein folding is a spontaneous process. The laws governing folding are still not well understood.Anfinsen's ribonuclease experiment. Denaturations.

“Spontaneous” means that folding can proceed without an external template when the conditions permit; it does not mean that every protein folds unaided, instantly, or into only one state. Folding is governed by a free-energy landscape shaped by sequence, solvent, temperature, pH, ligands, and cellular conditions. Some proteins fold with help from molecular chaperones, and some remain partly disordered or assemble only with partners. Kinetic traps and aggregation can compete with productive folding. [11]

In Anfinsen's classic ribonuclease A experiments, denaturation and reduction disrupted the enzyme's native structure and disulfide bonds; removal of the denaturing and reducing conditions allowed the purified protein to regain activity and its native disulfide pairing. The work supported the principle that sequence contains information sufficient for a protein's native conformation under suitable conditions. It does not imply that every protein folds independently in a living cell, or that the native state is always a single static structure. [12]

Denaturation is the loss of higher-order structure and activity caused by conditions such as heat, extreme pH, chaotropic agents, or detergents. Whether it is reversible depends on the protein and the conditions: some proteins refold when conditions are restored, while others aggregate or undergo irreversible chemical changes.

How to interpret structural models

Structural models are built from measurements, and each method samples molecules under specific conditions. X-ray crystallography infers atomic arrangements from diffraction by ordered crystals; NMR spectroscopy measures signals from molecules in solution and can describe ensembles; cryo-electron microscopy reconstructs structures from many frozen particles. Resolution, sample preparation, molecular flexibility, and the fitted model all affect which details can be interpreted confidently. Comparing methods and states can reveal motions that a single structure would hide. [10]

For DNA, a helix drawing is an explanatory model, not a complete account of its behavior in a chromosome. For RNA and proteins, a single static structure likewise cannot represent every conformational state, interaction, or cellular condition. Structural claims are strongest when the molecular context, experimental method, and limits of the model are stated alongside the coordinates or diagram.
