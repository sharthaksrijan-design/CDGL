Core process

Translation

Translation reads the codons in messenger RNA and uses them to assemble a polypeptide. The ribosome coordinates the message with transfer RNAs, which bring the amino acids specified by each codon.

The code and its interpreters

Codons establish the reading frame

The ribosome reads messenger RNA in groups of three bases called codons, moving along the message 5′ to 3′. Each codon specifies an amino acid or a stop signal. Translation usually begins at AUG, which encodes methionine (formylmethionine in bacterial initiation); start context determines which reading frame is used. Because the message is read as consecutive triplets, shifting the start by one base changes every codon downstream.

The genetic code is redundant: several codons can specify the same amino acid. It is also nearly universal, with some important variations, including in mitochondria. A stop codon does not specify an amino acid; it tells the translation machinery to end synthesis.

How might the genetic code have evolved?

No single hypothesis fully explains how the modern code arose. The stereochemical hypothesis proposes that some amino acids have direct chemical affinities for their codons or anticodons. The coevolution hypothesis links code expansion to the evolution of amino-acid biosynthesis pathways, with new amino acids acquiring codons related to those of their precursors. The adaptive hypothesis proposes that the code was shaped in part to reduce the harmful effects of translation errors or mutations. These ideas address different evidence and need not be mutually exclusive; the historical path remains unresolved. [Crick, 1968]

Ribosomes and transfer RNA

A ribosome has a small subunit that positions the messenger RNA and checks codon–anticodon pairing, and a large subunit that catalyzes peptide-bond formation. Each transfer RNA carries an amino acid at one end and an anticodon at the other. The anticodon pairs with the corresponding messenger-RNA codon.

Bacterial ribosomes are commonly described as 70S particles made of 30S and 50S subunits; eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes are 80S particles made of 40S and 60S subunits. The Svedberg unit measures sedimentation and depends on size, shape, and density, so the values are not additive. The small subunit decodes the message, while the large subunit contains the peptidyl-transferase center. Mitochondrial and other specialized ribosomes have additional variations. [Schmeing & Ramakrishnan, 2009]

Before use, an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase attaches the appropriate amino acid to its transfer RNA, using ATP. This “charging” step helps ensure that the amino acid delivered to the ribosome matches the anticodon. Some flexibility at the third codon position, called wobble, allows one transfer RNA to recognize more than one synonymous codon.

Initiation: choosing the start

Initiation brings the small ribosomal subunit, messenger RNA, initiator transfer RNA, and initiation factors together. Once the start codon is positioned correctly, the large subunit joins. This establishes the reading frame and places the initiator tRNA in the ribosome's P site.

The details differ across life. In many bacteria, the Shine–Dalgarno sequence in the messenger RNA helps align the start codon with the ribosome. In many eukaryotes, the small subunit is recruited near the 5′ cap and scans until it reaches a suitable start codon context. These are common strategies, not the only ways translation can begin.

Elongation: adding amino acids

Codon recognition

A charged transfer RNA is delivered to the ribosome's A site with help from an elongation factor. Correct codon–anticodon pairing promotes acceptance of the tRNA; mismatched tRNAs are more likely to leave before the next step. This selection, together with accurate tRNA charging, helps maintain fidelity.

Peptide-bond formation and movement

The growing polypeptide is held by the tRNA in the P site. The ribosome's large-subunit ribosomal RNA catalyzes transfer of that chain to the amino acid on the A-site tRNA, forming a peptide bond. The chain therefore grows from its N-terminus toward its C-terminus.

With energy from GTP, the ribosome then moves by one codon. The tRNA carrying the growing chain shifts from the A site to the P site, and the now-empty tRNA moves toward the E (exit) site and leaves. The cycle repeats, adding one amino acid at a time.

Termination and protein maturation

When a stop codon enters the A site, a release factor recognizes it. The factor promotes release of the completed polypeptide from its tRNA; ribosomal subunits, messenger RNA, and tRNAs can then be recycled. There is no matching stop-codon tRNA.

The new chain may begin folding while it is still being synthesized. Chaperones can help it reach a stable conformation, and additional steps may include cleavage, chemical modification, transport to a particular part of the cell, or assembly with other subunits. A translated chain is therefore not automatically a finished, functional protein.

Post-translational modifications expand the possible states of a protein. Phosphorylation can alter activity or binding; acetylation and methylation often affect protein interactions and localization; glycosylation changes protein properties and trafficking; and ubiquitination can mark proteins for degradation or redirect their interactions. These marks are installed and removed by regulated enzymes, so they are not simply encoded as one fixed amino-acid sequence.

Proteostasis is the set of processes that maintains the production, folding, localization, and turnover of proteins. Chaperones assist folding and limit inappropriate aggregation; the ubiquitin-proteasome system degrades many selected proteins, while autophagy can clear larger assemblies and damaged organelles. These quality-control routes determine how long a protein remains in the cell and complement the synthesis steps described here. See molecular chaperones and proteostasis and regulation of gene expression.

From sequence to cellular function

The order of amino acids influences how a polypeptide folds and which molecules it can interact with. Its final role depends on that structure and on the cellular context in which it is made, modified, and localized. Translation is thus a direct decoding of nucleotide sequence into amino-acid sequence, while protein function emerges through folding and further cellular processes.

See transcription for how messenger RNA is made, and molecular machinery for the components that carry out translation.

Translational regulation and measurement

Translation initiation is a major control point. During cellular stress, phosphorylation of eIF2α reduces the availability of the eIF2–GTP–initiator-tRNA complex and suppresses initiation on many messages, while selected transcripts such as ATF4 can be translated more efficiently. mTORC1 responds to nutrients and growth signals and can promote cap-dependent initiation by regulating 4E-BP proteins and other translation factors. These pathways alter protein synthesis rates without changing the underlying mRNA sequence. [Sonenberg & Hinnebusch, 2009]

Ribosome profiling sequences ribosome-protected RNA fragments to estimate which regions of a transcriptome are occupied by translating ribosomes at a particular time. It provides a powerful, genome-wide view of translation, but ribosome occupancy is not identical to the amount of completed, stable protein; it should be interpreted with protein measurements and other evidence. [Ingolia et al., 2009]

Experimental evidence: decoding codons

Nirenberg and Matthaei added synthetic poly-U RNA to a cell-free bacterial protein-synthesis system and observed production of a polypeptide made of phenylalanine. This linked the repeated UUU sequence to phenylalanine and established a direct experimental route for assigning codons. Later synthetic-RNA and biochemical experiments mapped the rest of the code. See the history chapter for the foundational experiment and Nirenberg and Matthaei, 1961.
