Context & applications

Evolutionary origins

How biological systems first stored, copied, and expressed information remains unresolved. Current models use evidence from chemistry, geology, comparative biology, and laboratory experiments to test possible routes.

Questions about early life

Any account of life's origin must explain how molecules became organized into systems that could persist, reproduce with variation, and undergo selection. For the central dogma, a narrower question is how sequence-bearing molecules became linked to catalysts and cellular compartments.

The evidence is indirect. The earliest organisms left no direct record of their molecular machinery, and geological evidence for life does not identify the exact chemistry by which it began. Origin-of-life research therefore compares hypotheses and tests whether proposed steps are chemically plausible, rather than claiming a complete reconstruction.

RNA-based hypotheses

The RNA-world hypothesis proposes that RNA played both information-bearing and catalytic roles before the modern division of labor among DNA, RNA, and proteins. RNA can store sequence information, form complex structures, and catalyze reactions; ribozymes and the RNA-rich catalytic core of the ribosome show that these capacities exist in present-day biology.

However, demonstrating that RNA can catalyze reactions today does not establish that an autonomous RNA-based life system existed. Researchers continue to investigate plausible prebiotic routes to nucleotides, nucleotide activation, RNA polymerization, copying accuracy, and the emergence of compartments. These are substantial open problems, and different RNA-world models propose different sequences of events. [Fine & Moses, 2025; Agmon, 2024]

Possible emergence of DNA and proteins

In modern cells, DNA is generally more chemically stable than RNA and is suited to long-term genome storage. Proteins can fold into a wider range of structures and provide diverse catalytic and binding activities. These properties help explain why DNA and proteins are effective in their present roles, but they do not prove the historical order or mechanism by which those roles arose.

One broad proposal is that RNA-based systems were gradually supplemented by peptides and then by specialized protein enzymes, while DNA became the principal genomic material. The transition would have required new synthesis and copying pathways, and likely proceeded through intermediate systems rather than a single switch. The exact chronology and selective advantages remain under investigation.

Alternative and complementary models

Other hypotheses emphasize the formation of compartments, mineral surfaces, metabolic reaction networks, or coupled cycles of chemistry and replication. These proposals need not be mutually exclusive with RNA-based models. A compartment could concentrate molecules; a surface or reaction network could support synthesis; and a genetic polymer could later enable more reliable inheritance. The challenge is to connect such plausible pieces into a continuous, experimentally supported pathway.

Evidence and open problems

Laboratory experiments can demonstrate that a proposed reaction occurs under specified conditions, but they do not by themselves establish that it occurred on early Earth. A strong model must account for realistic environments, access to reactants, competing reactions, and a route from chemistry to evolvable systems.

Active questions include how nucleotides were produced and concentrated, how early polymers copied themselves with useful fidelity, how catalysts and templates co-evolved, and when DNA and translation became established. The many modern roles of RNA help frame these questions, but the origin itself remains unsettled.
