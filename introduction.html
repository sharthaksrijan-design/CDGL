The Central Dogma of life

In a ever evolving world of living organisms, the different aspects of ones own molecular and genetic functions seem to communicate to each other in a peculiar yet effective manner. What is this form of communication? What constitutes biological information? How is it realised in ones own systems?

At a glance

The central dogma of life, abbreviated elsewhere as the CDGL, is a model of sequence-information transfer in molecular biology. Introduced by Francis Crick in 1957 and published in 1958, it concerns how sequence information is transferred between nucleic acids and proteins. It describes transfer pathways rather than positioning proteins as terminal receivers of all biological information. Of the nine possible transfer classes Crick considered, no established pathway copies a protein sequence back into a nucleic-acid sequence.

What is biological information?

Before we proceed, we need to understand what constitutes biological information and how it is represented within living systems. Crick defined information in said context as the precise determination of sequence of bases (in a nucleic acid) or amino acids (in a protein). To conceptualize this, cellular information is a chemical system or stimulus that directly defines the functioning of the concerned cell. The purpose of this information, quite simply, is to convey to the cell what is needed, what is expected, and what is not.

Why do organisms need to store and express information?

To understand, let us understand a simple scenario. In molecular biology, we rarely purchase proteins as they are. The purpose? Transportation, storage, and shelf life. Instead, we purchase the plasmids which, when expressed, provide the concerned protein. Protein does a great job at expressing itself, as it can directly affect living systems. However, it is a poor transfer medium for genetic information. As such, a requirement for a better molecule, which is stable, resists mutation, and can replicate effectively, is required. This is our DNA. Storage of information inherently requires stability while expression favors activity. To achieve both with a single molecule would be a tall requirement. Hence, a complex system of information transfer governs both stability and expression of the genetic material.

Genotype and Phenotype

Introduced by Wilhelm Johannsen in 1913, he defined the "genotype" as the genetic constitution of the organism. The phenotype, on the other hand, covers the aspects of an organism distinguishable by direct inspection. Phenotype is the expression of genotype, as is protein to DNA. In certain contexts, we might consider their relationships analogous.

Is information all that's needed?

It does need to be clarified that information is merely a sequence. For it to have any meaningful effect, there must lie a structure through which a sequence gives rise to a functional outcome.

Crick's sequence-transfer categories

Crick distinguished three kinds of transfer among DNA, RNA, and protein. “General” described transfers for which there was evidence: DNA to DNA, DNA to RNA, and RNA to protein. “Special” described transfers that were not part of the ordinary pathway but were known or considered possible: RNA to DNA, RNA to RNA, and DNA to protein. “Unknown” referred to protein to protein, protein to RNA, and protein to DNA. The labels describe the state of knowledge in the original framework; later work established RNA-to-DNA and RNA-to-RNA copying in biological systems. [Crick, 1970]

The important boundary is sequence-specific: no known machinery reads a protein's amino-acid sequence and uses it to synthesize a corresponding nucleic-acid sequence. Protein conformations can influence other proteins, and proteins regulate nucleic-acid processes, but these are not protein-to-nucleic-acid sequence copying. See limits of information transfer.

Biological information as sequence information

In this context, biological sequence information is the ordered identity of bases in DNA or RNA, or amino acids in a protein. Crick's use of “information” concerned the precise determination of one sequence by another during molecular transfer. A nucleotide sequence can therefore be treated symbolically, while the molecule's chemistry and cellular context determine how that sequence is copied, read, and acted upon.

Shannon information theory quantifies uncertainty in a set of possible symbols or messages; for a sequence, entropy depends on the frequencies and dependencies of its symbols. This is a useful mathematical description, but it does not by itself measure biological function, meaning, or fitness. A sequence's effect depends on its structure, expression, interactions, and environment, so information-theoretic measures must be tied to a defined question and dataset. [Shannon, 1948]

Experimental evidence: tracing information flow

Different experiments established different steps rather than proving one universal pathway at once. Meselson and Stahl used isotope labeling to distinguish models of DNA replication; Brenner, Jacob, and Meselson tracked a short-lived RNA intermediate between genes and ribosomes; Nirenberg and Matthaei used synthetic RNA in a cell-free system to assign a codon to an amino acid. Together, these experiments connect DNA copying, transcription, and translation while leaving open other biological processes.
